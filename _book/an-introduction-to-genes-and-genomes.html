Fantastic Genes and Where to Find Them

1 An Introduction to Genes and Genomes

1.1 What is a gene?

A gene is a segment of DNA that directs the production of a functional product. A significant number of eukaroytic genes are protein coding. Each protein-coding gene is used as a template to create an RNA transcript through a process called transcription. The RNA transcript is then processed into a messenger RNA (mRNA). The most notable change that occurs during RNA processing is that intron sequences are removed from the RNA transcript leaving behind exons. This processed mRNA is then exported from the nucleus and used as a template to make a protein in a process called translation (Figure 1.1). Proteins do most of the work necessary to maintain cell structure, function and behavior.

Figure 1.1: An overview of Gene Expression. A small segment of DNA (light grey) containing a single gene (orange) is shown. The RNA transcript is highlighted in green (exons) and dark gray (introns).


Importantly, the size of a gene is usually defined by where transcription begins and ends. In fact, the site of transcription initiation and transcription termination define both the gene size (in the genome) and the RNA transcript size (but not the mRNA size which is typically shorter).

1.2 What is a genome?

These 24,000 protein coding genes are distributed linearly along chromosomes2 (Figure 1.2).


Figure 1.2: This schematic represents a hypothetical segment of a chromosome with orange rectangles representing genes distributed linearly along the DNA segment shown.


One complete set of genes/chromosomes inherited from a single parent makes up what we call the genome (sometimes called the “haploid genome”)3. The haploid genome consists of 23 chromosomes in XX females and 24 in XY males. Each chromosome varies in length. For example, chromosome one is the longest. It is a double helix of 248,956,422 base pairs (bp) and is 82.7 mm in length 4 (Figure 1.3).


Figure 1.3: Actual length of the human genome when scale bar equals one centimeter.


In diploid organisms like humans, somatic cells (i.e. skin, neurons etc) harbor two complete sets of chromosomes. Thus, in total, there is just over two meters of DNA crammed into each somatic nucleus! The diameter of a mammalian nucleus is 6 microns or 0.006 of a millimeter (on average). How does all this DNA fit? First, the diameter of a DNA double helix is tiny (only 2 angstroms) but also each chromosome is carefully packaged by proteins in a systematic and sterotypical manner (Figure 1.4).


Figure 1.4: This image is modified from Uhler and Shivashankar, 2017. It shows the various levels of chromosome packing found in nuclei during interphase of the cell cycle. The first level of packing requires an octamer of histone proteins that assemble into a ball-like structure called a nucleosome. Naked DNA wraps around these octamers forming ‘beads on a string’. Additional levels of packing into chromatin fibers and topological associated domains requires additional proteins. Individual chromosomes (23 pairs in humans) are then carefully orgnanized within the cell nucleus in a cell type-dependent manner.
